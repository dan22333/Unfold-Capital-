Start here · the strong opinion

The verdict — what to build, and where the value actually lives

You asked for a strong opinion, not a survey. Here it is up front; the rest of the document is the evidence. Read this section alone and you have the whole argument.

◆ The one-paragraph answer

Build one thing: an LLM that constructs and types the economic-link graph itself — then trade the slow diffusion of news across the non-obvious links it finds. Do not build "an LLM that reads news and predicts stocks" (commoditised, decaying) and do not build "an LLM that simulates the whole market as a crowd of agents" (errors compound, nobody has made it pay). The defensible edge is narrow and specific: typed, directional, multi-hop links in low-attention corners of the market that FactSet/Bloomberg supply-chain tables and simple embedding-similarity both miss — validated strictly out-of-sample, net of costs, on a model whose training cut-off you control.

AYour fear, answered honestly

Your instinct — "funds already map company connections and propagate news across them, so what's left?" — is correct, and it is the right fear to have. The research bears it out: FactSet shipped a supply-chain customer-momentum signal (Mar 2026), State Street runs network-linked customer signals, and Man Group, Point72, Balyasny and Bridgewater all run agentic research over filings and news. Graph-based news propagation is table stakes. But "already mapped" is not the same as "already priced," and three specific gaps survive:

  • 1.The known link signal is lossy, and everyone trades the lossy version. A supplier's stock reacts to its customer's stock return. But a +20% customer move can mean higher end-demand, a buyback, a cost cut, or multiple-expansion — each implies a completely different consequence for the supplier. The compressed market signal throws that away. Recovering the mechanism (why did the customer move, and what does that specific cause do to the supplier's units, margin, and EPS) is information the price signal does not contain.
  • 2.The deepest mispricing sits in exactly the graph region an LLM is cheapest at. Ali & Hirshleifer (2020) found cross-firm spillover is strongest for complex and indirect linkages — the 2nd- and 3rd-hop implications that human desks don't bother to trace and that SIC/NAICS industry codes can't see. That is the one empirical fact that most supports your idea (§Signal or illusion).
  • 3.The links themselves are the scarce asset — and building them from text is genuinely new. Traditional desks buy a static edge list from FactSet. The frontier (Breitung & Müller, JFE 2025; text-based networks à la Hoberg & Phillips) shows LLM-built networks surface real, return-predictive links that industry codes miss. This — graph construction, not sentiment scoring — is where a fresh edge can still live.
✕ And where your fear is fully justified — the illusion risks

Four findings say the naive version of this trade is already dead or an artefact. Take them seriously; they shape the whole build:

  • ✕It may be one factor, not many. Ali-Hirshleifer shows customer, supplier, geographic, technological and industry spillovers largely collapse into a single shared-attention factor. "Multi-hop reasoning" may just re-derive a known, largely-arbitraged signal — you can't stack these alphas.
  • ✕The flagship version already decayed to zero. Pinchuk (2023) shows the canonical Cohen-Frazzini customer-momentum effect is much smaller post-discovery and roughly negative after 2005.
  • ✕It lives in the "dies-after-costs" quadrant. Link-spillover is high-turnover and concentrated in small, illiquid names — exactly the corner Novy-Marx & Velikov and Hou-Xue-Zhang show does not survive trading costs.
  • ✕Leakage is acute for links specifically. An LLM "reasoning" that Firm A supplies Firm B may just be recalling a memorised, post-hoc-known relationship and its realised co-movement. Three independent studies show telling the model to "ignore the future" does not work (§4).

BSo: what to build (and what to refuse to build)

✓ Build this

A "link factory + mechanism-aware ripple" engine:

  • 1A deterministic entity-resolution spine (every firm gets a stable ID) so every link is auditable and point-in-time.
  • 2An LLM that reads filings + news to propose typed, directional links ("A's tariff exposure raises B's input cost because B sources component X from A's region") — including 2nd/3rd-hop and cross-industry links the tables miss.
  • 3A mechanism classifier: for each shock, what kind is it, and what is its sign on the neighbour's fundamentals — not just "the neighbour's stock moved."
  • 4A ruthless validation harness built first (leakage tests, deflated Sharpe, net-of-cost) — the moat is discipline, not the model.
✕ Refuse to build (for the MVP)
  • ✕"LLM reads headline → predicts next-day return." Real but decaying fast (Sharpe 6.5→1.2 in three years as everyone runs it); it's a capability check, not a business.
  • ✕An agent-population market simulator (crowds of LLM traders). Errors compound past ~10 steps; no one has an audited live track record; the diversity collapses.
  • ✕A giant LLM-extracted "knowledge graph" as source of truth. Raw GPT-5 scores F=0.23 at identifying supply-chain links. Anchor to a registry; use the LLM for candidate discovery, not ground truth.
  • ✕Fine-tuning / RL / Lean-verified theses. Prompting + retrieval is enough; there's no verifier for "was this trade right," and you can't prove a future from axioms (§6).

CThe honest probability

On the published evidence, the base rate is against a large, durable, net-of-cost new signal. The most likely truthful outcome of a serious 12-week test is a small, capacity-constrained, decaying edge — real in-sample, mostly competed or costed away at scale. That is not a reason to skip it; it is the reason to build the validator first and size the ambition to tens, not hundreds, of millions of dollars of capacity. The one version of your thesis the literature has not refuted, and that no fund publicly runs, is the narrow one above: LLM-typed directional graphs over low-attention, complex, multi-hop links, proven leakage-free.

◆ The whole project in one sentence
Build a system that reads a shock and constructs the causal ripple across economically linked firms into a ranked, typed, mechanism-aware list of who will be hurt or helped — and prove, with tests that assume the model is cheating from memory until shown otherwise, that the ranking predicts future returns well enough, and cheaply enough, to make money after costs.

Part 2 · The evidence, for and against

Is the signal new — or an illusion?

This is the question you kept circling: "maybe it has no new signal, it just has the illusion of one." So we went looking for papers on both sides. The verdict: the underlying economics is bulletproof, the tradable edge is small and shrinking, and the LLM layer is the part most exposed to fooling yourself. Here is the whole case, explained.

2.1The support — economic links really do predict returns

The mechanism is one of the most replicated facts in empirical finance: information diffuses slowly across economically linked firms because investors have limited attention. Prices react fast to a firm's own news and slowly to news about firms it is linked to. Each paper below built a different kind of link and found the same drift.

▸ How these studies work, with a simple example

Take Cohen & Frazzini (2008), the founding paper. They read disclosures where a company names a customer worth >10% of its revenue (US firms must disclose this). That builds a map: this supplier depends on that customer. Then each month they go long the suppliers whose customer's stock did well last month, and short the suppliers whose customer did badly, and subtract the standard risk factors. Everyday version: if you know Ford had a great month and prices haven't yet moved the small firm that makes 40% of its revenue selling Ford one specific part, you buy that small firm. The drift earned ~150 bps/month gross. Is that good? Enormous on paper — but "gross," pre-2005, and, as we'll see, mostly gone now.

✓ The two facts that most support your idea

(1) Ali & Hirshleifer's kicker: the abstract states the effects are "stronger for complex and indirect linkages" — where "complex" = more connections/higher centrality, and indirect (2nd-order) links matter most for low-analyst-coverage stocks. It's supporting mechanism evidence (the headline is the unification), but it's real, peer-reviewed, and points at exactly the multi-hop, low-attention graph region an LLM can traverse cheaply and a human desk won't.

(2) Breitung & Müller (peer-reviewed, JFE 2025): networks built from LLM-generated business descriptions + embeddings (~63k firms, 67 countries) find competitors/suppliers that SIC/NAICS miss, and those links predict returns. This is the closest thing to published proof that the graph-construction step — not sentiment — is where LLMs add something real. Caveat (verified): the alpha is a gross 7-factor number concentrated in microcaps, attributed partly to limits-to-arbitrage, with no Sharpe or net-of-cost figures — so it proves the links exist, not that they're scalably tradable.

2.2The unification problem — it may be one factor, not ten

Here is the finding that should worry you most, because it attacks the core of "multi-hop reasoning finds many new links." Ali & Hirshleifer (2020) show that customer, supplier, geographic, technological and industry spillovers largely collapse into a single factor — connected-firm momentum driven by shared analyst attention. The links look different but are proxies for the same underlying thing: two firms watched by the same overloaded analysts move together with a lag.

! Why this matters — you cannot stack these alphas

If you "discover" a customer link, a tech link and a geographic link between two firms, you have not found three signals. You've found one signal three times. A strategy that claims to add them up is double- and triple-counting the same premium. The honest test (§10) is whether your LLM-found links are marginal to the shared-analyst factor — i.e. do they predict returns after controlling for it. If not, you've built an expensive re-derivation of a known, decayed anomaly.

This is the strongest, most convergent body of evidence, and it is quantitative. Four independent findings say the naive link trade is at or below zero net-of-cost today.

▸ In plain English — the "death quadrant"

Two things make an anomaly untradeable: you have to trade it often (high turnover eats you alive on spreads), and it only works in small, thinly-traded stocks (wide spreads, you can't short them, you can't size up). Link-spillover has both problems: the signal refreshes every time a linked firm gets news (high turnover), and the drift is biggest in the small suppliers nobody watches (microcaps). Novy-Marx & Velikov measured that this combination usually nets to zero. Pinchuk then went and measured the specific trade and found it already died around 2005. This is the empirical heart of your fear, and it is real.

✓ The honest counterweight — decay is real mispricing, not a mirage

Chen & Zimmermann (open-source asset pricing) replicate ~98% of clearly-significant anomalies and find publication bias shrinks returns only ~12% — so the ~50% decay is real mispricing being arbitraged away, not p-hacking. Why is that good news? Because it means the effect was genuine, and fresh mispricing can re-appear wherever attention is scarce and a link is newly formed or newly relevant. Your edge, if it exists, is being early to links the crowd hasn't priced yet — not harvesting the decayed textbook one.

2.4The LLM-return-prediction papers — thin, and mostly not what they seem

  • ›Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023) — GPT scores news headlines, predicts next-day returns. Famous "~90% accuracy" is the non-tradable instant reaction; the tradable drift is ~55–58% and concentrated in small/hard-to-short names. Their own follow-up shows the Sharpe decaying 6.5→3.7→2.3→1.2 from 2021 to 2024 as the tool spread. Good as a capability check; a warning as a business.
  • ›Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) — the benchmark. Neural nets beat linear models (VW Sharpe ≈ 1.35). But this is ML, not LLM — the bar any LLM strategy must clear to count as new.
  • ›Chen, Kelly & Xiu — LLM embeddings of news; reported Sharpe ≈ 4.6. Implausibly high for a real strategy; leans on microcaps. Treat as an upper bound, not a target.
  • ›BloombergGPT / FinGPT — capability benchmarks, not alpha papers. No trading Sharpe. Do not cite them as evidence LLMs make money.
  • ›Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2024) — GPT-4 beats the median analyst at earnings-direction from anonymised statements. Exciting idea, but the arXiv version was withdrawn for data inconsistencies — cite the idea, distrust the numbers.
◆ Part 2 takeaway — the balanced bottom line

Strong: the economics (slow diffusion across links) is bulletproof, and two peer-reviewed results say LLM-built networks and complex/indirect links carry real, still-mispriced signal.

Likely illusion: it's probably one shared-attention factor, the flagship trade already decayed to zero, it lives in the death-quadrant for costs, and the LLM layer is wide open to memorisation bias.

The narrow survivor: new alpha, if any, lives in LLM graph-construction (finding non-obvious, multi-hop, low-attention links the market hasn't priced) — not sentiment scoring — proven marginal to the shared-analyst factor, leakage-free, and net of costs. That specific claim is unproven but unrefuted. That is the whole reason to run the test.

Part 3 · The tool

What LLMs can and cannot do here

Before trading, an honest map of the instrument. Two capabilities are real and useful; one danger is so severe it dictates the entire design.

3.1Real capability #1 — reading and typing causal links

The most reliable thing an LLM does for you is read filings and news and turn "who depends on whom, and how" into structured data. Not sentiment — structure. Given a 10-K it can extract "Firm A buys component X from Firm B; a shortage of X would raise A's costs and delay A's product." That is a language task, and it is exactly the graph-construction step §2.1 identified as the defensible edge.

! But a raw LLM is unreliable at links unaided

The sharp warning: on a supply-chain-relation identification benchmark, zero-shot GPT-5 scored F=0.229 against 0.854 for a smaller model trained against a real graph (InterCorpRel-LLM, 2025). Read alone, a frontier model invents and misses links. The fix is not "a better prompt" — it is to use the LLM for candidate discovery and anchor every candidate to a deterministic registry and to disclosed facts (§9).

3.2Real capability #2 — extracting a tradable news signal (the Lopez-Lira result)

◆ Read this first — this is EVIDENCE, not the strategy

This result is measured at a next-day horizon. That is not what you'll trade (your horizon is 1–2 months, §5). It's here for two reasons: it's the cleanest proof an LLM contains tradable information at all, and it teaches the lesson "statistically real ≠ profitable." The logic that carries to your horizon: if an LLM has signal at 1 day, and the market is even slower to price indirect multi-hop links, then 1–2 months is where the edge is bigger and less contested.

Lopez-Lira & Tang gave GPT a news headline and asked "good, bad, or neutral for the stock?", then checked whether that score predicted the next day's return via a regression:

3.1 ri,t+1 = α + β · Scorei,t + (controls) + εi,t+1 ri,t+1 = the stock's return the day after the news. Score = the LLM's sentiment (−1, 0, +1). β = return per unit of sentiment. Result: β̂ ≈ 0.161, t = 6.80.
▸ Definition — the t-statistic (is it real, or luck?)

Any estimate is uncertain — rerun on a different sample and β̂ shifts. The t-stat divides the estimate by that uncertainty: how many "error-bars" from zero it sits. |t| > 1.96 → under 5% chance it's really zero (the famous p < 0.05); |t| > 2.6 → under 1%. A t of 6.80 is overwhelming — the odds of luck are astronomically small. Is that good? Statistically, yes: the news→return link is real. But a big t-stat does NOT mean the effect is large enough to profit from, will persist, or survives trading costs. Those are separate hurdles — and §2.3 shows the naive version fails them.

▸ Turning a hit-rate into money — the Sharpe ratio, plainly

On the tradable drift, GPT is right ~55–58% of the time — barely above a coin flip, which is normal for real signals. How does that become money? The Sharpe ratio is your return divided by your risk (annualised): how much reward per unit of stomach-churn. A tiny per-trade edge, repeated over hundreds of independent bets a year, compounds: edge per trade × √(number of trades) is the intuition (the √252 that turns a daily edge into an annual Sharpe). A Sharpe of 1 is good; 2 is excellent; above 3 is rare and usually means you've made an error or found something that won't last. The killer: subtract trading costs and the decay from everyone else running the same trade, and Lopez-Lira's own Sharpe fell from 6.5 to 1.2 in three years.

▸ On "imitating humans" — the Simile / Stanford result, explained

The headline behind Simile's ~$2B valuation (Park et al., 2024): interview 1,052 people for two hours, feed each transcript to a GPT agent, and the agent answers surveys ~85% as well as the person predicts their own answers two weeks later (a score of 1.0 would mean "as consistent as you are with yourself"). Impressive — but it predicts stated attitudes, one person at a time, and LLM crowds are shown to flatten real disagreement. A market is a crowd interacting, with money at stake. So: real enough to build a market-research company on, not a turnkey market simulator. Useful to you as inspiration for the "simulate futures" idea (§12), not as a trading engine.

3.4The central danger — memorisation & look-ahead leakage

This is the risk that killed the "knowledge-graph alpha" startups and the one your whole design must defeat. The model has memorised history. When you backtest on the past, it can "predict" what already happened because it remembers it — not because it reasoned. Your beautiful backtest is then fiction.

✕ Four studies, one conclusion: you cannot tell it to forget
  • ✕Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu 2025 ("The Memorization Problem"): LLMs recall exact pre-cutoff financial values verbatim, reconstruct masked names/dates, and defeat "don't use the future" instructions — then skill drops to zero after the cutoff. Their conclusion: LLMs cannot be trusted for economic forecasts over periods inside their training data.
  • ✕Sarkar & Vafa 2025 (ICML): ask GPT for "pre-2020 risk factors using only info then available" and it surfaces COVID-19. Instructing it to ignore the future doesn't work.
  • ✕Glasserman & Lin 2023: GPT-sentiment backtests are inflated by a "distraction effect" from knowing the famous company — anonymising the name reduces it (a diagnostic and a partial fix).
  • ✕Ludwig, Mullainathan & Rambachan 2026: never trust a closed model's stated cutoff; valid LLM prediction requires provable "no training leakage." The burden is on you.
✓ The four defences (this is why the build looks the way it does)
  • 1Test only after the cut-off. Use an open-weight model with a documented training date and run the strategy strictly on data the model could not have seen — or, best, a point-in-time model (ChronoBERT/ChronoGPT, pretrained only on text available up to each date).
  • 2Anonymise names and dates. If the link-alpha survives when "Apple" becomes "Company 7," it's reasoning, not name-recall.
  • 3Falsify aggressively. Placebo tests, label shuffles, and a "lookahead propensity" audit that checks the edge does not correlate with how memorable a firm-date is.
  • 4Prefer open weights so the cut-off and training data are auditable — the whole reason for open models is inspectability, not (as an earlier draft assumed) pruning.
◆ Part 3 takeaway
The LLM's job is narrow and real: read text → propose typed, directional links → flag the mechanism. It is not a forecaster and not a crowd simulator. And because it has memorised the past, every result is guilty of leakage until proven innocent — which is why the validator (§10) gets built before the signal.
Part 4 · The opportunity

The strategy, where it survives, and the money

Given the tool and the evidence, which market do you actually test, and how much money is realistically in it? The rule that governs everything: an edge only survives where arbitrage is hard.

4.1The rule — edge lives where attention is scarce and arbitrage is costly

▸ In plain English — limits to arbitrage

If a mispricing were easy to exploit, a hedge fund already would have, and it'd be gone. So durable edges hide where exploiting them is hard: small stocks with wide spreads, names too illiquid to size up, positions you can't easily short, and horizons long enough that you carry real risk while you wait. This is not a bug — the difficulty is the moat. It's also the trap: the same illiquidity that preserves the edge is what eats it in costs. Your job is to find the sweet spot — links diffuse slowly enough to trade, in names liquid enough to trade cheaply.

Part 5 · The decisions you asked about

What you actually need — CLT, graphs, RL, Lean, explainability, world-models

You listed the techniques you keep hearing about and asked, one by one, do I need this? Here is a grounded answer for each — plain-English definition, the 2025–26 state of the art, and a blunt need nice skip verdict for a solo MVP. The through-line: your edge is economic, not architectural. Two unglamorous things move the needle — a structured link graph and rigorous inference. Most of the flashy stack you should admire and skip.

5.1Statistical inference / CLT need — build first

▸ Plain English

A Sharpe ratio, a t-stat, a backtest return — these are sample statistics, not truths. The Central Limit Theorem tells you they wobble, and the wobble shrinks only like 1/√(sample size). With a few years of data a Sharpe of 1 often can't be told apart from 0. And if you try 100 strategy variants and keep the best, its gorgeous backtest is partly luck — the more you try, the luckier the winner looks.

This is the single most important item on the list, and it is non-negotiable. As a solo researcher with no risk committee, rigorous inference is the only thing between you and elaborate self-deception. Concretely you need: Fama-MacBeth + Newey-West (test whether the premium is real in the cross-section, robust to correlation), the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (haircut your Sharpe for the number of trials and non-normality), and Probability of Backtest Overfitting via CSCV (how likely your winner is a fluke). Bailey & López de Prado showed that with 5 years of data, trying ~45 configurations almost guarantees an in-sample Sharpe of 1 whose true value is 0. Track your trial count honestly. It's cheap (open-source code exists) and it's where the whole literature lives.

5.2Entity / knowledge graph need — this IS the edge

▸ Plain English

Make firms nodes and economic relationships edges (customer→supplier, competitor, tech-peer). If Apple reports a demand shock, its suppliers should move — but prices lag, so the graph lets you trade the spillover. Entity resolution = attaching a stable ID (LEI, PermID, ticker) so "Apple," "Apple Inc." and AAPL are one node. GraphRAG = building a graph + summaries over a whole corpus to answer "connect-the-dots" questions plain vector search can't.

Yes — but the tradeoff resolves cleanly, and it's important you get it right: do not choose "LLM" vs "graph." Use the LLM/embeddings for candidate discovery, and a deterministic registry for resolution and validation. Two facts pin this down: raw GPT-5 scores F=0.23 at identifying supply-chain links (so LLM-only is unreliable as ground truth), while Breitung & Müller show LLM embeddings do surface real links worth testing. So build a deterministic entity-resolution spine (every link auditable and point-in-time — without it your graph is built on hallucinated identifiers and the backtest is meaningless), seed the graph with disclosed customer-supplier data, and add LLM-embedding candidate links validated out-of-sample. skip full Microsoft GraphRAG for the MVP (heavy indexing cost; its strength is thematic synthesis, not your need) — reach for LazyGraphRAG only later if you want corpus-level summaries.

5.3AI agents & reasoning models need (selectively)

▸ Plain English

Ways to make an LLM do things: tool-use (it emits get_price("AAPL") and your code runs it), ReAct (Thought→Action→Observation loop), reasoning models (o1/R1 — trained to think in a long hidden chain; more test-time compute → higher accuracy on hard problems), self-consistency (sample many reasoning paths, take the majority vote), and multi-agent debate (copies argue toward consensus).

Need: tool-use/function-calling (the plumbing to fetch data and run your own validators), plus a reasoning model with self-consistency as your signal-confidence layer — sample N rationales for a directional call, use the vote-share as a soft probability. The literature specifically says the fancier schemes don't beat this: multi-agent debate is largely deflated (2025 studies: it often fails to beat single-agent chain-of-thought while burning far more compute). nice a deep-research agent as a research assistant (drafting "why might these two firms be linked"), never as an autonomous signal source. skip long-horizon autonomous loops — the frontier reliably completes only ~1 hour of human-equivalent work (METR), so scope tasks small.

5.4Explainability / interpretability skip (as a trust mechanism)

▸ Plain English

Ways to see why a model answered: attention maps, feature attribution (SHAP/LIME), mechanistic interpretability (reverse-engineering the internal algorithm), and sparse autoencoders (SAEs) that decompose tangled activations into readable single-concept "features."

No — ex-post outcome verification is enough for a trading signal. A signal's validity is settled by out-of-sample PnL and the statistics in §5.1, not by understanding the model's internals. Trusting a signal because you traced its circuit is a category error — and even the labs building this (Anthropic, DeepMind) have publicly dialled back the promise: DeepMind deprioritised SAE research after SAEs underperformed simple linear probes on a real task. nice, later one legitimate exception: SAE features as an input representation for defining "similar/linked" firms beat GICS sectors in one pairs-trading paper. That's using interpretability tooling as a feature source, not as an explanation you rely on.

5.5Verifiable-reward RL / fine-tuning skip for alpha

▸ Plain English

RLHF = train on human preference rankings (the ChatGPT recipe). RLVR = replace the human with a deterministic checker — reward = 1 if the math/code/SQL answer is correct (DeepSeek-R1's method). LoRA = fine-tune cheaply by training tiny adapter matrices on one GPU.

No, not for the MVP. Your bottleneck is fresh, correct information (a retrieval problem) and disciplined validation (a stats problem) — neither is solved by RL. And the fatal mismatch: there is no cheap deterministic verifier for "was this trade right" — the market takes months to grade you and the signal is noisy, so RLVR (which needs a dense, cheap verifier) simply doesn't fit alpha. Worse, 2025 work shows RL mostly "sharpens" abilities the base model already had rather than teaching new ones. nice, narrow, later one defensible use: a cheap LoRA (~$15) for a structured extraction task — reliably pulling supplier/customer relations or XBRL fields from filings (FinLoRA-style). That's a data-engineering win, not an alpha engine. Note: your instinct that "verifiable = check the chain once the future arrives" is the right framing — but that's an ex-post evaluation, not an RL training loop.

5.6Formal verification with Lean skip (a category error)

▸ Plain English

A proof assistant (Lean, Coq) lets you write math so precisely a computer mechanically checks every step; if it type-checks, it's guaranteed correct. Example: prove n + 0 = n for all naturals.

No — and there's a principled reason, not just a budget one. Formal verification proves that conclusions follow from axioms. An investment thesis is an empirical, probabilistic claim about an uncertain future — there are no axioms from which "AAPL will outperform" can be derived. Formalising your thesis in Lean would either certify the arithmetic of your assumptions (garbage in, garbage out) or be a category error. LLM+Lean is a real, exciting frontier (DeepMind's AlphaProof hit IMO-silver) and formal methods are used in finance — but only for deterministic objects: exchange matching-engine correctness (Imandra), contract payoff DSLs, smart-contract verification. A targeted search for "formalise a trading thesis in Lean" returns nothing, because it isn't a thing. If it ever appears in your memo as a pillar, that's the load-bearing caveat to cut. (Your ex-post "verifiable" idea from §5.5 is the right version of this instinct.)

5.7World models / simulating the future concept yes · naive no

This is your original idea, so it gets its own full section later (§12). The short verdict: simulating likely futures as an idea-generation funnel is legitimate; trusting the LLM to be the simulator is not (errors compound — the ACL-2024 world-simulator study measures GPT-4 at ~59.9% single-step accuracy and notes that, by their own compounding estimate, that falls to 0.59910 ≈ <1% over 10 steps; LLMs don't out-forecast elite humans; the flashiest claims are contaminated by leakage). Use it to generate candidate spillover hypotheses, then validate them with §5.1.

Part 6 · Beyond the supply chain

The other links — rates, geography, politics, macro

You asked whether the idea extends past supply-chain ripples to interest rates, geography, politics and the like. It does — and these are exactly the cross-domain links §2.1 said an LLM is uniquely good at, because they live in narrative text, not in any industry-code table. But there's a discipline: add them as typed edges and conditioning variables, not as a second free-floating strategy.

▸ In plain English — two ways a "link" can enter your model

(1) As an edge between two firms: "Firm A and Firm B both depend on Taiwanese fabs" (geographic/input link) or "both are hurt by a steel tariff" (policy link). A shock to the shared exposure ripples to both. (2) As a conditioning variable (a "regime knob"): the level of rates, or the policy-uncertainty index, changes how strongly a given shock propagates. A demand shock ripples differently in a rate-tightening regime than a loosening one. Both are legitimate; keep them separate in the model so you can test each.

! The one macro trap that will silently ruin your backtest — vintages

Macro series like GDP, payrolls and CPI are revised after release. If your 2018 backtest uses today's revised GDP number, you're using data that did not exist in 2018 — instant look-ahead leakage, and it looks like alpha. Also note: plain FRED's terms forbid using its data to train ML/LLMs — use FRED numbers as cited model inputs, pull training-grade history from the primary agencies.

6.2The discipline — don't let scope explode

Each new channel is a new set of trials, and §5.1 taught you what trials do to your Sharpe. The right way to add rates/geo/politics is pre-committed and typed: decide the edge types and conditioning variables before you look at returns, register them, and test whether each adds predictive power marginal to the supply-chain core. If a channel doesn't earn its keep after the deflation penalty, drop it. The goal is one richer graph, not five strategies you'll be tempted to cherry-pick among.

▸ The division of labour — LLM vs deterministic code

The design principle that keeps you honest: the LLM does the fuzzy language work; deterministic code does everything numeric, dated, and auditable. The LLM reads text and proposes "A → B, type = input-supplier, direction = cost-increase, confidence = 0.7." Code resolves A and B to stable IDs, checks the edge against disclosed facts, stamps it with the as-of date, propagates the shock along the graph, ranks the names, applies risk controls, and runs every statistical test. If a number can be computed deterministically, it must be — never ask the LLM to do arithmetic you can audit.

8.2The pipeline, end to end

  • 1Ingest & resolve (code): pull filings (EDGAR) and news (GDELT); resolve every mentioned entity to a stable ID via the identity spine (§7.2).
  • 2Build the graph (LLM + code): seed hard edges from disclosed ≥10% customers; LLM proposes additional typed, directional candidate edges from text; code validates, dates, and weights them. Every edge carries an as-of date — the #1 backtest killer if missing.
  • 3Detect the shock (code + LLM): an 8-K, an earnings surprise, a news event on a source node. LLM classifies the shock type and sign.
  • 4Propagate the ripple (LLM-scored, code-executed): for each neighbour, estimate the sign and magnitude of the fundamental effect (units, margin, EPS) — and let confidence decay with graph distance (2nd hop less certain than 1st).
  • 5Score vs consensus (code): compare the implied fundamental change to the current analyst consensus. The gap is the tradable signal — the market hasn't repriced yet.
  • 6Construct the portfolio (code): rank, neutralise risk factors jointly (not sequentially), size by confidence and liquidity, cap turnover.
  • 7Validate relentlessly (code): every leakage and significance test in §10, on every run.

8.3The corpus — "structured back to the entities"

Your instinct to keep a corpus of all past news/filings indexed to entities is correct, with one refinement: the unit of storage is the (event, company, timestamp) tuple, not "one file per company." For each event, freeze the information set exactly as it existed at that timestamp — the graph as-of then, the company's state, the evidence text, the LLM's estimated response and confidence, the consensus at the time, and later, the realised outcome. This event panel is what you run statistics on, and freezing it per-event is what prevents look-ahead leakage from creeping in through a "helpfully updated" field.

▸ Handling new / sparse-history companies — shrinkage

You don't need 10–20 years per stock. Use cross-sectional learning: a newly-public specialty manufacturer inherits priors from peers with similar industry, customer concentration and input exposure. Formally, estimate = λ·(company-specific) + (1−λ)·(peer prior), with λ small when history is short and rising as evidence accumulates — the same shrinkage logic used to tame noisy beta estimates.

8.4Methodology — the shape of the test

  • ›Cross-sectional panel, not one model per firm. Pool all events; learn the response function across the panel.
  • ›Time split, never random split. Train on the earliest period, validate on the middle, and keep the most recent period completely untouched for one final test.
  • ›Abnormal returns via CAR (cumulative abnormal return — the stock's move minus what its risk factors predict), with Fama-MacBeth t-stats computed on events, not rows.
  • ›Purge & embargo around each event so overlapping windows don't leak.
  • ›Beat every baseline that's simpler than you (next box) — the bar for "the LLM added something."
! The baselines you MUST beat (or the complexity isn't earning its keep)
  • 1Customer-momentum only (the FactSet-style signal).
  • 2Network-centrality-weighted customer momentum.
  • 3The shared-analyst factor (§2.2) — your links must be marginal to this.
  • 4A single LLM + RAG summary with no explicit graph propagation.
  • 5The graph with direct (1-hop) effects only — does multi-hop actually add value, or just noise?

Part 9 · The proof

Proving it's real — metrics, significance, and the leakage gauntlet

This is where projects like yours live or die. A plausible narrative is not evidence. This section is the machinery that separates a real signal from luck and from the model cheating off its own memory — and it's the part you build first, before any signal exists.

9.1The metrics, defined

▸ Information Coefficient (IC) — is the ranking any good?

The IC is the rank correlation between your predicted ranking of stocks and their actual next-period returns. +1 = perfect, 0 = coin flip. Is a small number good? Yes — real single-name equity ICs are tiny, typically 0.02–0.05. That sounds like nothing, but repeated across hundreds of names and many periods, an IC of 0.03 is a genuine, tradable edge. Anything above ~0.1 on a real single-name signal is a red flag for leakage, not a triumph.

Alongside IC: the net long-short return and Sharpe (after costs), turnover (how often you trade — the cost driver from §2.3), and the decay curve (how fast the edge fades after the signal fires — if it's gone in two days you can't trade the 4–8 week horizon).

9.2Is it signal or luck? Count events, not rows

▸ The most important statistical idea in the whole document

Say a chip shortage ripples to 40 suppliers and they all drift up. That looks like 40 independent confirmations of your theory. It is not — it is one. One shock hit 40 correlated names. Treating them as 40 independent data points is like judging a coach by the 11 players on the field as if each were a separate season. The honest count is the number of independent shocks, E — and that is a much smaller, more sobering number. This is why you run Fama-MacBeth (estimate the effect period-by-period, then test the series of estimates) rather than pooling every stock-row into one regression that pretends they're independent.

9.3The deflated Sharpe — punishing yourself for trying many things

▸ Why your best backtest is probably too good

If you try many strategy variants and report the best one's Sharpe, you've cherry-picked the luckiest — even from pure noise, the maximum of N random Sharpes grows roughly like √(2·ln N). The Deflated Sharpe Ratio subtracts exactly this expected "luck bonus" for the number of trials you ran (and corrects for non-normal returns). The practical rule from §5.1: honestly log every variant you test, and let the deflation haircut your headline number. This is also the single strongest argument for building one pre-committed strategy rather than fishing — fishing inflates N and destroys your own significance.

9.4Do you even have enough data? An honest power analysis

The naive calculation (treat every stock-row as independent) says you need ~8,700 observations and you have plenty. That's the trap from §9.2. Deflating for the fact that names within a shock are correlated, the real requirement is in independent shocks E:

▸ How to live with a small E — narrow in scope, wide in the panel

You can't manufacture more history, but you can make each shock count for more: pick a narrow, well-defined shock type (e.g. major-customer earnings surprises) so the events are clean and comparable, then widen the panel of linked names per shock so each event is richly measured. And forward-test live: every month that passes adds fresh, uncontaminated shocks to E — the most credible evidence of all.

11.1Simulating futures vs. using exact data — is there anything there?

▸ In plain English — what "simulate the future" would even mean

Instead of reacting to a shock that already happened, you'd ask the model to roll the world forward: "given today's state, enumerate the plausible branches over the next two months and their consequences." You then bet where the market's implied probabilities look wrong — especially asymmetric spots where a cheap position pays off big in a branch the market underweights.

The honest read has three layers:

  • 1As a forecaster, the LLM is not good enough to bet on directly. Superforecasters still beat top LLMs (p<0.001 on clean benchmarks), LLMs-as-text-simulators lose the plot past ~10 steps (~59.9% single-step accuracy compounds to an estimated <1% over 10 steps), and several "superhuman forecasting" claims turn out to be temporal leakage. So "the LLM predicts the future" is not the edge.
  • 2Your "already in the price?" worry is mostly right for obvious scenarios. Liquid options already price the branches everyone can see. If the LLM's scenario is one any analyst would list, it's priced. The only room is where enumerating the branch requires chaining several non-obvious links — which loops you straight back to the link-graph thesis: the value isn't "predict the future," it's "trace a consequence the crowd hasn't traced yet."
  • 3Asymmetric bets are real but hard to harvest. The mispricing you'd need lives in tails, options are expensive to hold, and the branch has to actually resolve inside your horizon. Possible as a later extension; a bad place to start because it multiplies the leakage and small-E problems.
✓ The one legitimate role for simulation — an idea funnel

Use scenario generation to propose candidate spillover trades ("if this customer cuts orders, which three under-covered suppliers are mispriced, and through which mechanism?"), then feed every candidate into the §9 validator. The LLM's imagination is a hypothesis generator; the statistics are the judge. That keeps the creative upside of your original idea without betting money on the model's forecasts.

11.3The MIT thesis — "Population AI" (Ayush Chopra, 2026)

The iceberg.mit.edu thesis is Ayush Chopra's MIT Media Lab PhD, Population AI: Modeling Societies of Interacting Agents. Its one-line thesis: intelligence in a population emerges not from how smart each agent is, but from how they interact. It's about pandemics, supply chains and labour disruption — not markets — but three of its ideas map cleanly onto yours, and its epistemic posture is a gift.

▸ What he actually built, and what's novel

Two things: AgentTorch — an engine for agent-based simulations that are both massively scalable (millions of agents on GPUs) and differentiable (the whole sim is written as tensor operations, so you can take gradients through it) — and Large Population Models, the framing that studies emergent phenomena (epidemics, supply-chain cascades, labour shifts) arising from interaction, not from any one agent.

What's genuinely novel: (1) differentiability at population scale — the real contribution: ordinary agent-based models are black boxes you calibrate by brute force; a differentiable one calibrates by gradient descent and gives sensitivity analysis in a single pass. (2) LLMs as agent "cognition" showing adaptive behaviour a fixed rule can't (the pandemic-fatigue result). (3) archetypes to make LLM-agents affordable (with the caveat they re-introduce coarse assumptions). It's method and machinery applied to pandemics/labour — not markets, and some widely-quoted figures I'd verify at source; the technical approach is the solid, transferable part. Open-source code: github.com/AgentTorch/AgentTorch (Python/PyTorch, AGPL-3.0 — same copyleft caveat as MiroFish; borrow the ideas, don't necessarily take it as a dependency).

▸ The big idea (and a note on what's verified)

Model a whole population: each agent has a state, a decision rule ("cognition," which can be a frozen LLM given a structured prompt), and structured interactions with neighbours ("coordination"). Because the whole simulator is built to be differentiable, you can do fast sensitivity analysis — measure how the collective outcome changes if you nudge an input — and calibrate the model to real data from a single pass.

Honesty flag: an earlier draft of this section attributed a named "sensitivity sharing" mechanism and a vivid supply-chain "Beer Game" result (specific cost/shock numbers) to the thesis. On re-checking against Chopra's published papers those specifics could not be verified and are removed. What is verified appears below.

✓ What's genuinely verified — and worth borrowing
  • 1The Lucas-critique argument for LLM agents (verified). His papers say it directly: policy/regime effects "cannot be predicted solely from historical data when agents can adapt their behaviour." Models fit to the past are not regime-invariant. This is textbook economics and the principled case for your "simulate a regime absent from history" instinct.
  • 2LLM agents can represent adaptive behaviour (verified). The real experiment: inject "it has been N+30 weeks of the epidemic" into agent prompts and they become less willing to isolate, worsening the outbreak with transmissibility unchanged. That decouples behaviour change from the underlying dynamics — something a fixed statistical rule can't do. Note the honest scope: faithful LLM-agent sims are demonstrated only at 25–1,000 agents.
  • 3Archetypes to scale — with a verified outperformance result. You can't LLM-query millions of agents, so cluster into a few representative "archetypes" and query once per type. In his AAMAS-2025 paper this ran 8.4M agents with ~400 LLM calls and beat both a hardcoded-rule baseline and the naive per-agent-LLM approach at forecasting (infection error 95 vs 2,915 vs 4,312; ~30× better), at ~95% less runtime. Honest framing: the same model (GPT-3.5) is used throughout — the win is the structure, not a small model beating a big one — and archetypes re-introduce some coarse assumptions.
  • 4The epistemic posture — build a "capability/exposure map, not a forecast." The thesis's applied work is framed as correlational, not causal, validated against independent data and labelled "exposure, not outcome." That is exactly the honest framing your signal should adopt — the antidote to narrative hallucination.

The trading move I suggested — propagate a firm's sensitivity (how its EPS would move if a linked firm is shocked), not its reported level — is a reasonable idea, but it is my synthesis, not a mechanism the thesis provides. The thesis is about pandemics, supply chains and labour; it never touches asset prices or trading.

And the bigger synthesis — "an LLM system would beat history-trained quant models at regime changes and produce tradable alpha" — is, per the independent verification, overreach / an untested hypothesis. The premises hold (models break at regimes; LLM agents can represent adaptation), but combining them into market alpha is unproven: simulating an adaptation is not forecasting the right one, markets are adversarial and reflexive (if the reasoning were reliably right, prices would already reflect it), and — the crux — it cannot be backtested, so in its pure form it is closer to gambling than to a validated edge. Treat it as a research direction, sized small, never the core.

12.1Can you be as big as the big funds? The capacity/edge law

▸ In plain English — the iron law

Capacity scales with market depth; edge scales inversely with how many eyes are on it. You can push billions through liquid markets (large-caps, futures, FX, rates) — but they're the most-watched, so the edge per dollar is thin. You get a fat edge only in small, ignored corners — which can't absorb size. You rarely get both. That is why the earlier "~$30–150M" number is a property of the small-cap strategy, not a limit on your ambition.

! The tell — Renaissance's Medallion

Medallion returned ~66%/yr for 30 years — the best record in history — yet it's capped at ~$10B and has refused outside money since 2005. Why cap the best fund ever? Its edge is in short-horizon inefficiencies with hard capacity limits — push more and the trades move prices and the edge dies. The tell: RenTec's other funds, the ones open to outsiders and built to be big, are mediocre — because to absorb billions they had to go liquid and long-horizon, where the edge is thin. Same geniuses: the small fund is spectacular, the big one is average. Great edge and huge AUM are usually mutually exclusive at the strategy level.

So how are the giants big? Not one huge signal — four things: (1) breadth (Citadel/Millennium run hundreds of independent, capped "pods"); (2) liquid instruments (billions of capacity each); (3) many horizons + leverage; (4) diversification math — dozens of modest, uncorrelated edges combine into one high-Sharpe, high-capacity book. Bigness = summing many capped edges, not owning one uncapped one.

✓ Your scaling ladder — the factory is a signal factory

This reframes your asset. It isn't the one small-cap ripple trade — it's a machine that manufactures many decorrelated link-spillover signals. That's the path to size:

  • 1Start small-cap — highest edge, proves the machine (~$30–150M).
  • 2Widen to liquid expressions of the same links — the industry-portfolio version of link-spillover (Menzly-Ozbas) is the most tradable and dodges the microcap cap: same idea, 10–100× capacity, lower Sharpe per unit.
  • 3Add channels, horizons, asset classes, leverage (equity, credit, options; the rates/geo/politics edges of §6). Now capacity aggregates across many bets.

Do that and there's no structural reason you can't run a multi-billion book — the size comes from breadth × liquidity × diversification, which the factory produces.

! The tension in that ladder (verified, don't skip)

The known, human-legible link (shared-analyst / connected-firm momentum) works in large-caps and survives costs — great for capacity. But the novel, LLM-found links (Breitung-Müller) so far show their alpha concentrated in microcaps, gross of cost. So going liquid for capacity pushes you toward the more-competed links — and your "new-links" edge must prove it still adds value there, marginal to the shared-analyst factor. That is precisely the thing the test in §10 must establish.

▸ Wait — isn't this backtestable? The spectrum (a correction to "you can't")

Backtestability is not yes/no — it's three tiers, and Bridgewater lives in the middle one:

  • 2Bridgewater's regime bets — weakly backtestable. Rare events (inflation regimes, rate cycles, debt crises) recur a few times per century per country. So the earlier "you can't backtest it" is strictly true only for tier 3. Most useful regime bets are tier 2 — rare but with analogs, which is exactly why the LLM's analog-mining earns its keep: more and better historical/global analogs strengthen the tier-2 test.

12.6Two clarifications that keep coming up

▸ "Build the simulator, don't be it" — in detail

Paradigm A — LLM is the simulator (generative). You keep the world in the model's head and ask it to produce each next state in words: "inventory=100, demand=50, order 30 → next state?" → it writes "inventory=80…", you feed that back, ask again. Why it fails: each step is only ~60% right; feed its own slightly-wrong output back in and errors compound multiplicatively (~0.610 ≈ <1% after 10 steps). It also can't guarantee accounting identities, can hallucinate, and isn't auditable. The ACL-2024 study measured exactly this and found it unreliable.

Paradigm B — LLM builds the simulator (neurosymbolic). The LLM's job is one-time: help you write the explicit model — code, equations, graph, parameters. Then deterministic code runs it forward. "Neurosymbolic" = neural (LLM, good at fuzzy language) + symbolic (explicit code/logic, exact and auditable): the LLM turns messy text into symbolic structure, the engine executes it reliably. Why it doesn't drift: the code applies the same exact rules every step — no per-step generation error to accumulate; you can enforce identities, bounds, conservation, correct-by-construction. AgentTorch (Chopra) is this — agents as differentiable tensor operations you can also take gradients through for calibration; an LLM plugs in as one component (a decision rule), but the scaffolding (who's linked, how state updates, how shocks propagate) is explicit code. The LLM feeds the simulator; it isn't the simulator.

For marketsDo this instead
"Simulate 2 months and tell me what happens to semis" (LLM-as-simulator → drift, hallucination)Use the LLM to build the pieces, run them deterministically ↓
1 · Entity graphLLM extracts candidate typed/directional links from text; you store them as explicit, dated data
2 · Response functionsLLM proposes "input cost +10% → supplier margin −Y bp" as a parameterised rule; you encode & calibrate it to history
3 · Propagation engineDeterministic code walks the graph hop-by-hop, confidence decaying with distance — same rules every run, auditable, no drift
4 · Run & validateYou roll the deterministic model forward over scenarios and test the outputs statistically

This is the same LLM-vs-code split as §8.1, applied to simulation — and it's how you legitimately "unroll the future": change a knob on your deterministic model (e.g. rates +200 bp) and re-run it, rather than asking the LLM to hallucinate 2027.

▸ Can Lean verify real-world / future scenarios? No.

Lean verifies that conclusions follow from axioms — pure logic. "2+2=4" is true by the rules and machine-checkable. "AAPL will rise" is not entailed by any axioms — it depends on empirical facts and an uncertain future, which no theorem prover can settle. Lean can confirm a math proof is correct or that a pricing formula is coded correctly (deterministic, logical objects) — it cannot tell you whether a scenario will happen. The right tool for "is my edge real" is probabilistic/statistical validation (backtests, calibration), not a proof assistant. Lean stays a skip.
