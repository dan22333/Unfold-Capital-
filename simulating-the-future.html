Simulating the future, and hunting an Ackman trade

What Ayush Chopra actually built (AgentTorch), in plain English and through worked examples — why a mechanistic simulation can answer "what if?" when a statistical model can't, how "sensitivity" becomes a convexity detector for asymmetric bets like Ackman's 2020 CDS trade, how you calibrate a model on the past for a shock that never happened, whether it's already priced in, the AI trends making this buildable now, what Stanford's CS329A teaches that you'd use, and a concrete MVP to see if you can catch it.

Part 0 · Start here

What did this guy actually build?

Forget the jargon for one paragraph. Here is the whole thing in kitchen language.

Ayush Chopra (MIT Media Lab) built a piece of software called AgentTorch that lets you make a toy copy of a world — a city, an economy, a supply chain — out of many little "agents" (people, companies) that follow rules and affect each other. Simulations like that have existed for decades. He added two new powers that together are the actual contribution:

  • 1You can train the toy world like an AI model. Instead of hand-guessing the world's settings ("how contagious is this disease?"), you show it real data and it auto-tunes its own settings to match reality — the same math that trains a neural network.
  • 2You can let an LLM be the "brain" of each agent. So an agent doesn't blindly follow a frozen formula; it reasons about its situation and adapts — like a real person or a real management team would.

His proof-of-capability was a COVID model of 8.4 million synthetic New Yorkers: it ran a real epidemic, let people adaptively decide whether to isolate, and tuned the disease's parameters to fit real case data automatically. That's it. He did not build anything about markets — but the machine he built is the exact kind of machine your fund idea needs. The rest of this doc is about why, and where it breaks.

▸ The one sentence to remember

He built a "what-if machine": describe a world and a shock, press play, and watch the consequences ripple through — and because it's built out of real cause-and-effect (not curve-fitting on history), you can ask it about shocks that never happened before. Your fund is a bet that this machine, pointed at the economy, can find asymmetric trades before the market sees them.

Part 1 · The "frozen rule" problem

Frozen equations vs. an LLM that adapts

You asked about "being stuck with a rule." This is the cleanest place to see why the LLM matters. It's the difference between an agent that follows a fixed formula forever and one that changes its mind as the situation changes.

1.1The old way: a frozen rule

In a classic agent-based model, every agent obeys a rule the modeller wrote once, by hand, and it never changes. For example:

# a frozen behavioural rule — written once, fixed forever
if cases_nearby > threshold:
    isolate = True        # always, no matter what
else:
    isolate = False

That rule is a guess, and worse, it's a rigid guess. Real people don't behave like that. Eight months into a pandemic, with the same case numbers, people got tired and stopped isolating — "pandemic fatigue." A frozen rule can never produce that: feed it the same input, it forever spits out the same output. It has no notion of "I'm exhausted," "the mood has shifted," "the government just changed the message." The behaviour cannot adapt to a situation the modeller didn't foresee.

▸ Why this is a real problem for markets, not just epidemics

Swap "isolate?" for a company's decision. A frozen rule says "if input costs rise 10%, cut capex 5%." But a real CFO's response depends on the whole context — is this a temporary spike or a regime change? are competitors cutting too? is credit still open? A fixed coefficient can't capture that judgement, and judgement is exactly where the non-obvious, non-priced consequences live.

1.2The new way: the LLM is the agent's brain

Chopra's move: instead of a frozen if, ask an LLM. Give it the agent's situation in words and let it decide:

# the LLM decides the behaviour, in context
prompt = f"""You are a NYC resident, month {t} of the pandemic.
Local cases: {cases}. You've been isolating for {months} months.
Your job {job_status}. Do you isolate today? Reason, then answer."""
isolate = llm(prompt)   # can say "no" in month 8 that it said "yes" in month 2

Now the same case count can produce a different decision depending on fatigue, job status, messaging — because the LLM carries a rich, human-like model of how a person actually reasons. Chopra's verified result: in a "pandemic fatigue" experiment, LLM-driven agents reproduced the adaptive drop-off in isolation that a fixed rule structurally cannot. That is the whole point of putting the LLM inside the simulation.

▸ The honest catch — the LLM does NOT run the numbers

Crucial subtlety (and your agenttorch-explained.html already nailed this): the LLM supplies the direction and shape of behaviour ("fatigue makes isolation less likely"), but it sits outside the math that gets tuned. The actual magnitude — how much less likely — is still a number the simulation calibrates against data. So it's LLM for judgement + gradient descent for the numbers, not "the LLM decides everything." If you let the LLM invent the magnitudes, you get confident nonsense.

Part 2 · The deepest question

"He can build causality?" — why a mechanism gives you cause-and-effect

This is the most important idea in the whole document, so we go slowly. Why can a simulation answer "what if X?" honestly, when a normal statistical/ML model can't?

2.1Correlation model vs. mechanism model

A statistical / ML model

Learns "when X happened in the past, Y usually followed." It is a fit to what already happened. Ask it "what if X were different?" and all it can do is extrapolate the old correlation — which quietly assumes the world still works the way it did in the training data.

A mechanistic / agent model

You write down the actual chain of causes: shock → each entity reacts → their reactions hit the next entity → … When you change X, you re-run the chain and it computes the consequence through the mechanism, not by remembering history. It can answer about an X that never occurred.

▸ The everyday example that makes it click

Suppose you have years of data and notice: every time ice-cream sales rise, drownings rise. A correlation model, asked "what if we ban ice cream — do drownings fall?" would happily say yes. Wrong — the real cause is summer heat drives both. A mechanism model, which encodes "heat → swimming → drownings" and "heat → ice cream," answers correctly: banning ice cream does nothing. Only a model that encodes the real causal arrows can answer a "what if" it never saw. That's what "counterfactually valid" means, and it's why Chopra can claim causality where a curve-fit can't.

2.2Why economists call this the "Lucas critique"

There's a famous result in economics (the Lucas critique) that Chopra explicitly invokes — and it's the same idea. A pattern you measured under one regime breaks the moment the regime changes, because the agents react to the new regime. "People spent X% of a tax cut last time" won't hold if people now expect the cut to be temporary. So for any policy or shock counterfactual, you need a model of the mechanism (how agents decide), not a fitted historical relationship. A differentiable agent simulation is exactly a machine for building such structural models — that's his defensible claim.

▸ The boundary — causality is only as good as your mechanism

"Causally valid" is conditional on the mechanism being right. If your causal arrows are wrong, the model gives you a confident, precise, wrong counterfactual — arguably more dangerous than an honest correlation. So the whole game is: get the mechanism approximately right, and stress-test it hard. This is why the fund's edge is a proprietary, well-validated mechanism, not "we have a simulator."

Part 3 · Scenario roll-forward

Walking the cascade — "who blows up first?"

You quoted the line: "demand shock → revenue → margins → cash flow → default probability → spreads → who blows up first." You asked: is this from his work? Here's the honest split.

Shock
Demand drops / lockdown of size S
Revenue
Each firm's sales fall — by how much depends on the firm
Margin
Fixed costs don't fall → margins compress faster than revenue
Cash flow
Thin cash + high debt = trouble servicing interest
Default risk
Probability of missing a payment rises
Spreads
Credit spread / CDS widens → who blows up first
▸ What's his, and what's yours (be precise)

The machinery is his. "Take a shock, push it step-by-step through a network of interacting entities, and read the outcome at the end" is exactly what AgentTorch does — his COVID model pushes an infection through a contact network; his toy economy pushes spending through earn→consume→employ. The finance cascade above is MY mapping of that machinery onto credit — he never built revenue-to-default anything. So: verified the engine exists and does cascades; synthesis that you can point it at corporate credit. Don't tell an investor Chopra proved a credit model — he didn't.

Why a static statistical signal can't do this. A signal like "customer stock went up 20%, so buy the supplier" gives you one compressed number. It can't tell you which firm in the chain is the fragile one, why, or when the stress arrives — because it never modelled the mechanism. The cascade does, which is the entire meaning of "fundamental analysis of the future": you're reconstructing the chain of fundamentals a shock will set off, not pattern-matching a past correlation.

Part 4 · Counterfactual branching

Branching the future — with a concrete example

"Because it's mechanism, not correlation, you can enumerate branches." Let's make that physical: what Chopra could branch, and what you would branch.

4.1What HE built & could find

His COVID model is a branching machine. Freeze the city at month 3 and run two worlds that differ by one intervention:

Branch A — lift lockdown now

Contacts rise → transmission rises → but the LLM-agents see cases climbing and some self-isolate anyway → the curve bends in a way a fixed rule would miss. Output: a specific infection + mortality trajectory.

Branch B — hold lockdown 3 more months

Different contact pattern → different curve → different behavioural fatigue by month 6. Output: a second trajectory you can compare, apples-to-apples, to Branch A.

That comparison — two worlds identical except for one lever — is a genuine counterfactual. It's the thing you cannot get by staring at historical data, because history only ran one of the branches.

4.2What YOU would build & could find

Same machine, entities = companies, edges = supplier / customer / competitor / lender links. Freeze the economy at a date and branch on a macro shock:

BranchWhat propagates through the firm graphWhat you read off per company
RecessionDemand ↓ across consumer-facing nodes → suppliers → lendersΔrevenue, Δmargin, Δdefault-prob, Δspread
Soft landingMild slowdown, credit stays openSmall moves; most names barely budge
Credit freezeRefinancing shuts → high-leverage, short-maturity names hit firstA few names blow out; the rest survive

What you're hunting in that table: a company whose outcome is nearly unchanged in the soft-landing and recession branches but catastrophic in the credit-freeze branch — and whose insurance (CDS / puts) is cheap because the market is pricing the boring branches. That mismatch is the Ackman shape. Branching is how you find it systematically instead of by gut.

▸ Honest limit — branching maps consequences, it doesn't pick the branch

The engine tells you what each branch does. It does not tell you which branch will actually happen or its probability. That judgement — "the credit-freeze branch is more likely than the market thinks" — is the human's, and it's where the real conviction and risk live.

Part 5 · The exciting one

Sensitivity = a convexity detector

This is the piece that should genuinely excite you for the Ackman case, because it turns "find the asymmetric bet" from art into a search you can automate.

5.1What the gradient literally is

Because the whole simulation is differentiable, after one backward pass you get, for every entity at once, the number:

the sensitivity ∂(entity's value or spread) / ∂(shock) In words: "if the shock gets a little worse, how much does this company's value/spread move?" One backward pass gives it for thousands of companies simultaneously — no re-running the sim once per company.

A small, flat sensitivity = a boring name; a little more stress barely moves it; the market has it about right. A steep sensitivity = the name reacts violently. But the real prize is the second derivative — the curvature:

convexity ∂²(value) / ∂(shock)²   ≫ 0 This is "how fast the sensitivity itself accelerates." Big curvature = below some threshold almost nothing happens, but past it the entity falls off a cliff. That non-linear, cliff-edge shape is convexity.
▸ Why that shape is exactly an Ackman trade

Ackman's 2020 CDS position had a payoff like: if the world stays fine, you lose a small, fixed premium; if the world breaks, you make a fortune. That asymmetric, cliff-edge payoff is high convexity. So a machine that computes curvature across every entity and ranks them is literally a machine that surfaces "cheap-premium / huge-tail-payoff" candidates — the thing Ackman found by hand on investment-grade CDS. You'd be searching the whole graph for the next one instead of eyeballing it.

5.2The honest caveats

  • ✗The curvature is only as trustworthy as the calibrated mechanism. Wrong pass-through model → wrong convexity ranking.
  • ✗High convexity ≠ a good trade. You still need (a) the shock to be plausible, (b) the market to be under-pricing it, and (c) a cheap instrument to express it. The detector finds candidates; it doesn't close the deal.
  • ✗Everyone can compute Greeks. Option convexity is already priced. Your edge is only in convexity the standard models miss — the kind that comes from network/supply-chain propagation a single-firm model never sees. That's the specific, defensible claim.
Part 6 · Your sharpest objection

"It never happened before, so how can we calibrate on the past?"

You pushed back on "calibrated on past stress (2008, 2015, 2020)" by saying: the future shock is new, we can't know it. You're half right, and the other half is the key insight.

▸ The resolution — you calibrate the physics, not the event

You do not calibrate on "COVID." COVID never happened before, agreed. You calibrate the mechanism's response coefficients — e.g. "per 10% drop in cash flow, how much has a firm like this historically widened in spread?" That relationship — the physics of how stress turns into default — recurs across 2008, 2015, 2020, even though the trigger was different each time (housing, oil, virus). You learn the gravity, then drop a new object.

▸ The hurricane analogy

You've never seen this hurricane. But you calibrate how water flows downhill and where a city floods on past storms — that mechanism is stable. Then you simulate this new storm's specific rainfall over that calibrated terrain. The storm is novel; the hydraulics aren't. Same with markets: the shock is novel; the pass-through from cash-flow stress to default is far more stable.

▸ When this fails — and how to respect it

Sometimes the mechanism itself breaks in the new regime — 2008 had bank-to-bank feedback loops that a model calibrated on calm years wouldn't contain. So: (1) calibrate across multiple, diverse stress regimes, not one; (2) grade every bet by how much it relies on a recurring mechanism vs a genuinely unprecedented structure. Your sim-alpha.html already has this ladder: recurring-mechanism bets are tier-1, backtestable; truly unprecedented regime bets are tier-3, closer to gambling. Know which one you're making.

Part 7 · "Is it already priced in?"

Model-implied vs. market-implied — the gap is the trade

You asked what "contrast model-implied default distribution vs market-implied (CDS-implied)" means, and where this stands today.

7.1Two independent estimates of the same thing

The chance a company defaults can be estimated two ways, and comparing them is the whole game:

Market-implied

A CDS price is insurance against default. Its spread backs out a default probability (roughly: spread ≈ default-prob × loss-given-default). So the market is constantly telling you its estimate of each firm's default odds — for free.

Model-implied

Your mechanistic simulation also produces a default probability, by pushing a scenario through revenue → cash flow → default. This estimate knows things the market's single number compresses away — like a supply-chain shock two hops upstream.

the signal Gap = Pmodel(default) − Pmarket(default) Gap ≫ 0 → your model sees danger the market doesn't → buy protection cheap (the Ackman setup). Gap ≪ 0 → market too scared → sell protection. The gap, out of sample, is the candidate mispricing.
▸ The Ackman trade in this one language

Feb 2020: his model (a human one) said "COVID cascade → corporate defaults spike." The CDS market's implied default probability was still near-calm. Enormous positive gap, and the protection was cheap. He bought it. When the market's implied probability caught up to his model's, the gap closed violently in his favour. Your engine is an attempt to find that gap systematically, across thousands of names.

7.2Where this stands nowadays

  • •The idea is old and respected. "Structural" default models (Merton, then Moody's KMV / distance-to-default) have compared model-implied vs market-implied default for decades. So the frame is proven and institutional — not a crank idea.
  • •It's also hard and mostly efficient. Those models are noisy, and the CDS market is usually right. Simple versions of this gap are well-arbitraged. You will not beat it with a textbook Merton model.
  • •What's genuinely new — and your shot. Standard structural models look at one firm in isolation. They do not model a shock propagating through the supply-chain / customer network into a firm's cash flow. An LLM-built, network-aware mechanism could see a default driver that single-name models miss. That is the frontier and the only place the edge can live.
  • •Post-2020 reality check. Everyone now runs tail-hedge and convexity desks; cheap convexity is rarer than it was for Ackman. The obvious version is crowded. The non-obvious, network-driven convex node is not — which is exactly why the network model, not the single-name model, is the point.
Part 8 · A guardrail, stated plainly

Why you don't just ask the LLM to predict the future

It's tempting to think "the LLM simulates the future, I bet on what it says." The evidence says: don't. Here's why, and what to do instead.

  • ✗Humans still beat it. Trained superforecasters outperform top LLMs on clean forecasting benchmarks (statistically significant). The LLM is not a better crystal ball.
  • ✗It loses the plot over many steps. As a step-by-step world-simulator, per-step accuracy compounds — roughly 60% right each step becomes <1% after ~10 steps. Long roll-forwards drift into fiction.
  • ✗Many "superhuman forecasting" claims are leakage. The model had already seen the future in its training data. Treat every such claim as guilty until the knowledge cutoff is proven to be before the event.
So what IS the LLM for

Machine surfaces, human judges.

Use the LLM to build the mechanism and the graph (who depends on whom, which direction a shock pushes), to reason about the consequences of a scenario you hand it, and to generate candidate convex/mispriced names. Then a human decides which scenarios are real, whether the market is asleep, and when to pull the trigger. The LLM is the tireless analyst that maps consequences; it is not the portfolio manager.

Part 9 · Practical

Should you download the repo and run it?

Short answer: yes — to learn, not to build on. Running one model end-to-end will make everything above click far faster than more reading.

▸ Do this (an afternoon)

1. Clone github.com/AgentTorch/AgentTorch.
2. Start in tutorials/ and examples/, and use the MockLLM so you don't need to wire a real model.
3. Run the macro-economics model and watch it print loss.backward() and the per-parameter gradients — that's the whole "train an economy" idea, live.
4. Then read one transmission substep to see message-passing move a shock across the graph.

▸ Watch out

Don't start in the two trainer.py files — they have hardcoded paths and a stale import (AgentTorch.helpers → now agent_torch) and will just error.
Licence: it's AGPL-3.0 — fine as a reference design to learn from, but do not link it into a proprietary fund product. Treat it as a teacher, not a dependency.

▸ The build takeaway

For your v1 you re-implement the ~20% that matters — a differentiable message-passing graph + gradient calibration — cleanly in your own PyTorch (a few hundred lines). The millions-of-LLM-agents machinery is a v2 upgrade, not a prerequisite. Run his repo to understand the mechanism; own your version to trade it.

Part 10 · The tailwind

AI trends that make this buildable now

Three years ago this was a research fantasy. Several fast-moving trends have turned the hard sub-problems into off-the-shelf capabilities. This is the "why now" you'd tell an investor.

  • 1Reasoning models got real. LLMs went from autocomplete to genuine multi-step reasoning (chain-of-thought, "thinking" models). Reasoning through a causal cascade — the core of your engine — is exactly what they've become good at.
  • 2Test-time compute + self-consistency. You can now trade dollars for reliability: sample an answer many times and take the consensus. This directly de-noises the LLM's link/sign estimates — the same "15 samples per call" your cost model already assumes.
  • 3Verifiers & process rewards. The field learned to check reasoning step-by-step, not just the final answer. That's the antidote to your #1 failure mode — narrative hallucination — because you can machine-verify each hop of a claimed cascade.
  • 4Tool use + execution feedback. Agents now ground their claims in real data and code (query a database, run the numbers, read the result) instead of vibing. Your mechanism's magnitudes come from data, not from the LLM's imagination.
  • 5Open-weight frontier models. Strong open models (DeepSeek, Qwen, Llama-class) mean you can fix and audit the knowledge cutoff — the single most important leakage guardrail — and self-host cheaply. You can't do point-in-time honesty on a closed API whose training data you can't see.
  • 6Agentic search / deep research. Automated multi-hop retrieval over filings and news makes building the entity graph — the scarce asset — tractable at scale.
▸ The trend that does NOT save you

None of this makes LLMs forecasters. The tailwind makes the mechanism-reasoning and verification reliable — not the prediction of which scenario occurs. Ride the first; keep a human on the second.

Part 12 · The test

An MVP to see if you can actually catch it

The point of an MVP is a fast, honest go/kill: does the mechanism contain real predictive information the market missed, with zero hindsight? Two versions — start with the easy one.

AThe starter MVP — reactive link-spillover easiest · tier-1

This is the cheapest thing that can teach you something real, and it's the honest first rung toward the Ackman ambition.

1 · Pick shocks
~100 clean events: a supplier issues a big guidance cut on date D
2 · Build graph pre-D
Open-weight LLM, cutoff before D, reads pre-D filings: "firm X depends on S"
3 · Propagate
1–2 hops, one learnable decay coefficient → predict linked firms' 4- & 8-week return
4 · Calibrate
Fit the decay on earlier shocks; test on strictly later ones
5 · Score
OOS hit-rate / information ratio vs 3 baselines

Beat these three baselines or you have nothing: (a) the market itself, (b) a plain-LLM sentiment score on the same news, (c) the naïve customer→supplier trade. Go/kill: a stable, economically meaningful edge on the held-out later period, after costs. Compute: hundreds to low-thousands of dollars. Time: a few weeks.

BThe ambition MVP — "re-discover the Ackman trade" harder · closer to the dream

This tests the scenario/convexity engine directly, as a postdiction under a strict cutoff — can the machine find the asymmetric bet from information available before the event, not with hindsight?

  • 1Pick a past stress episode with a datable trigger (e.g. early 2020 COVID, or a sharp sector shock). Freeze all information to a cutoff just before it was priced.
  • 2Hand the engine the scenario ("a broad demand + credit shock of size S") — you supply the branch; the engine maps consequences. This is fair: Ackman also chose the scenario.
  • 3Rank every entity by (i) convexity ∂²value/∂shock² and (ii) the model-vs-market gap in default probability, using only pre-cutoff data and a cutoff-locked LLM.
  • 4Check the tape. Did the engine's top-ranked convex / most-underpriced names actually blow out? Would the cheap convex position on them have paid?
  • 5The leakage test — the whole point. Re-run with the cutoff removed. If it only finds the trade with hindsight, you have leakage, not alpha. Catching it before the cutoff, beating a plain-LLM and a naïve baseline, is the real signal.
What "catching it" honestly means

Surfaced before the event, from a locked information set, beating the simple baselines — after costs.

Not "the story sounds right." Not "it found it in hindsight." The bar is: with the clock frozen before the shock, the engine puts the right convex / underpriced names near the top, and that ranking would have made money net of trading costs — and a plain-LLM summary and the naïve baseline would not. Clear that on a couple of independent episodes and you have something worth funding. Fail it, and you've saved yourself a year — which is exactly what a good MVP is for.
